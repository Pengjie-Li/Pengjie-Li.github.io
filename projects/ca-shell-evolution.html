PLB 2024 Nucleon knockout

Shell evolution in neutron-rich calcium

The first measurement of the deeply bound neutron 0f7/2 orbital in exotic calcium isotopes — a direct window into how the nuclear tensor force reshapes shell structure far from stability.

An atomic nucleus is often drawn as a dense little ball of protons and neutrons. But on the inside, those particles are arranged into shells — much like the electron shells of an atom. When a shell is completely filled, the nucleus becomes especially stable; physicists call such a filled shell a “magic number.” For decades this simple picture explained ordinary, stable nuclei very well.

Venture away from stability, however, and the rules start to bend. As you pile more and more neutrons into a nucleus, the forces between the particles shift, and the shells can rearrange themselves — a phenomenon physicists call shell evolution. Old magic numbers can fade and new ones can appear. Figuring out how this reshuffling happens is one of the big open questions in nuclear physics.

Why calcium?

Calcium is the perfect place to study this. Its 20 protons form an unusually stable core, so researchers can watch the neutron shells change without the extra complication of a shifting proton structure. For years, experiments mapped the outer neutrons of calcium isotopes with great precision. But one crucial layer stayed hidden: the deeply bound orbitals buried far inside the nucleus, where the strongest fingerprints of shell evolution — carried by a force called the tensor force — are expected to show up.

One orbital in particular, the neutron 0f7/2, sits deep below the surface in neutron-rich calcium. In isotopes such as 54Ca and 56Ca it is “deeply bound”: removing a neutron from it costs a lot of energy and leaves the remaining nucleus violently excited. Ordinary techniques simply could not reach it.

The experiment

To look deep inside, we used a reaction called quasi-free knockout. We sent rare-isotope beams of 54Ca and 56Ca, traveling at about 216 MeV per nucleon, into a target of liquid hydrogen at the RIKEN Radioactive Isotope Beam Factory in Japan. A proton from the hydrogen strikes a neutron inside the calcium nucleus and knocks it out — a bit like a perfectly aimed billiard shot. We caught the ejected neutron with large detector walls (NEBULA and NeuLAND) and measured the leftover fragment with the SAMURAI spectrometer.

The crucial clue was the neutron’s momentum. Just as the spray pattern of shattered glass reveals how it was hit, the momentum “fingerprint” of the knocked-out neutron tells us which orbital it came from. By measuring both the neutron’s momentum and the energy of the leftover nucleus, we could reconstruct exactly where that neutron had been living inside the calcium nucleus.

What we found

The data revealed excited states in 53Ca at about 5.5 MeV and in 55Ca at about 6.0 MeV. The momentum pattern of these states matched the prediction for an f-wave — an orbital with angular momentum l = 3, a particular shape of motion — and clearly ruled out the alternatives. In other words, we had the first direct experimental evidence that the neutron 0f7/2 orbital really exists in neutron-rich calcium, just as theory had long predicted but never been able to confirm.

Our numbers line up well with two leading theories: the GXPF1Bs shell-model calculation and modern ab initio computations. Interestingly, the same orbital behaves very differently in nickel (with 28 protons), underscoring how special calcium’s proton core is as a clean testing ground.

Why it matters

This work turns quasi-free knockout into a powerful new tool for peering into the deepest layers of the nucleus — layers that were invisible to conventional methods. The result was published in Physics Letters B, and it gives nuclear theorists a sharp new benchmark for testing how the strongest forces in nature build the structure of the atomic nucleus.
